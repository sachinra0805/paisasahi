The formula we use (same for every lane):
EMI = P × r × (1+r)n ÷ ((1+r)n − 1), with r = annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100 and n = tenure in months. Reducing-balance, monthly rests — identical maths to our EMI calculator, run three times.
Assumptions: each rate is held fixed for the full tenure; fees and charges are excluded — add them to the costliest column of your own comparison before deciding. Floating rates will move; this page compares starting rates honestly, and no more than that.

Why lenders quote rates and hide gaps

"Only 0.5% more" sounds like nothing at a sales desk. Over 20 years on ₹50 lakh, that "nothing" is roughly ₹3.5–4 lakh. Lenders know the tenure multiplies quietly in the background; the comparison above puts the multiplied number in front of you, where it belongs. When two offers differ mainly in processing fee but not rate, the fee usually wins the argument — when they differ in rate, the rate almost always does.

Using this for a balance transfer

Already hold a loan? Set the amount to your outstanding balance and the tenure to your remaining tenure. Put your current rate in one lane and the transfer offer in another. The interest gap is your gross saving — subtract the new lender's processing fee and any prepayment penalty on the old loan. If the gap still wins by a wide margin, the switch is real; if it's close, it's marketing.

Common questions

How much does a 1% rate difference actually matter?

On ₹50 lakh over 20 years, about ₹7–8 lakh of total interest. The bigger the loan and the longer the tenure, the more each tenth of a percent is worth.

Compare by EMI or by total interest?

For the same amount and tenure they rank offers identically. Total interest is the truer cost — a lower EMI bought with a longer tenure can cost far more overall.

Is switching lenders for a lower rate worth it?

Only when the interest gap beats the switching costs — processing, valuation, legal, and any prepayment penalty. This page gives you the gap; subtract the costs before deciding.

Fixed or floating?

This page holds rates fixed to show the arithmetic. A floating rate's future path is unknowable — compare the starting gap, then judge your own room to absorb rises.

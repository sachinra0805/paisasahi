How big should your emergency fund be?

"3–6 months of expenses" is the standard advice — and it's wrong for half the people who hear it. A freelancer with two kids and a home loan needs more than a government employee with no EMIs. This sizer adjusts for your life.

The one-line answer: most households need 3–12 months of essential spending set aside. Your number rises with EMIs, dependents, and income instability — this tool computes yours and shows how fast you can build it.

The formula (published, like everything here):
Months = 3 + (EMI > 30% of essentials ? 1 : 0) + (dependents ≥ 2 ? 1 : 0) + (self-employed or freelancer ? 1 : 0), capped at 12.
Target = months × (monthly essentials + monthly EMIs) · Build time = target ÷ monthly savings.
Government/PSU jobs start at the 3-month base because income is the most stable; variable incomes get the extra month because dry spells are normal, not exceptional.
Illustrative example. Arjun spends ₹50,000/month on essentials, pays ₹25,000 in EMIs (that's 50% of essentials → +1 month), has 2 dependents (+1 month), and freelances (+1 month). Base 3 + 3 = 6 months. Target = 6 × ₹75,000 = ₹4,50,000. Saving ₹15,000/month, he builds it in 30 months — which is why starting today beats starting "someday."
Assumptions & limits: "essentials" means non-negotiable spending only; the tool assumes no existing liquid savings and excludes investments you won't touch. The job-type adjustments are rules of thumb, not actuarial science. Revisit the number yearly or after any big life change — marriage, kids, a home loan, a job switch.

Where should the fund actually sit?

Two tests: can you reach it within a day or two, and will it still be there in a market crash? That rules out equity and long lock-in FDs. What passes: a separate savings account (a sweep-in or flexi-deposit earns a bit more while staying liquid), or a liquid mutual fund with instant redemption. "Separate" matters more than the exact vehicle — money parked in your daily spending account gets spent.

When is it okay to break the glass?

Income shocks and health shocks: job loss, a medical emergency, an urgent repair you can't postpone. Not vacations, not the latest phone, not a wedding you "had to" attend. The discipline that makes the fund work is the same discipline that decides when to touch it. And the rule after any withdrawal: rebuilding the fund outranks every other financial goal until it's whole again.

Should I kill debt first or build the fund first?

Sequence it: one month of essentials first (a starter buffer so small shocks don't become new debt), then attack high-interest debt like credit cards and personal loans aggressively, then build the full fund. Doing the full fund before clearing 36%-interest debt means you're earning 6% while paying 36% — the math never works.

Questions, answered

Where should I keep my emergency fund in India?

Somewhere safe and reachable within a day or two: a separate savings account, ideally with a sweep-in or flexi deposit, or a liquid mutual fund. Not in equity, not locked in long fixed deposits with penalties, and not in the account you spend from daily.

When is it okay to use the emergency fund?

For income or health shocks: job loss, medical emergencies, urgent home repairs. Not for vacations, gadgets, or weddings. And rebuild it immediately afterwards, before resuming other investing.

Should I clear debt first or build the emergency fund first?

Do both in order. Keep a small starter buffer of about one month of essentials first, then attack high-interest debt aggressively, then build the full fund. An emergency with zero buffer usually becomes more debt.

Isn't keeping cash idle a waste?

It is insurance, not investment. The return is not being forced to borrow at 36% or redeem long-term investments at the worst possible moment. Judge it against the cost of the alternative, not against equity returns.

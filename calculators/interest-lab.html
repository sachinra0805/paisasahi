Interest Lab: Simple vs Compound

Same principal, same rate, same years — two different rules. For the first year they look like twins. Then compounding starts earning interest on interest, and the gap never closes again.

Updated: October 2026
The one-line answer: simple interest pays only on your original money; compound interest pays on your money plus everything it has already earned. Time is what turns that small definitional difference into lakhs.

The formulas we use:
Simple: Value = P × (1 + r × t ÷ 100)  ·  Compound: Value = P × (1 + r ÷ (100 × k))k × t
where P = principal, r = annual rate %, t = years, k = compounding periods per year.
Assumptions: one deposit, no additions or withdrawals; rate constant for the whole period; no tax or TDS. Real FD returns are trimmed by tax at your slab — the gap between the two rules, however, survives tax, because both sides are taxed.

Where each rule actually applies to your life

Compound, working for you: FDs (quarterly compounding is the Indian bank norm), EPF, PPF, and any investment where returns stay invested. Compound, working against you: credit card balances and any loan you underpay — the unpaid interest joins the principal and starts charging you rent too. That's the mechanics behind our minimum-due trap. Simple: mostly penalty interest, some short-term arrangements, and exam questions. If a product quotes you a rate, the first honest question is always: simple or compound, and how often?

Common questions

What's the actual difference?

Simple interest is computed only on the original principal. Compound interest is computed on principal plus accumulated interest — interest on interest. Close in year one, decisively different by year ten.

How often do Indian FDs compound?

Typically quarterly. That's why an FD's effective yield is slightly above its headline rate — try the quarterly setting above and compare with yearly.

What is the Rule of 72?

72 ÷ compound rate ≈ years to double your money. At 8%, about 9 years. It only works for compound interest, because simple interest never accelerates.

Where is simple interest used?

Some short-term loans, penalty and late-payment calculations, and certain quoted conventions. Most deposits and virtually all loans compound — which is why this distinction is worth money.

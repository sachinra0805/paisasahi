CIBIL score: the 90-day improvement plan

Updated: October 2026

The short answer: a CIBIL score (300–900) is built by five behaviours: paying on time — the single biggest factor — keeping card utilisation under 30%, letting old accounts age, applying for new credit rarely, and holding a healthy mix. Follow the phased plan below and most people see movement by day 60–90. No agent, no paid "score repair," no shortcuts — because there aren't any.

What actually moves a CIBIL score?

Bureaus don't publish their exact scoring weights, but across every major scoring model the hierarchy is consistent. In plain order of importance:

  1. Payment history — the heavyweight. One 90-day late payment can undo a year of good behaviour. Autopay is not optional advice; it's the foundation.
  2. Credit utilisation — how much of your available limit you use. Under 30% is the widely cited healthy zone; under 10% is ideal.
  3. Length of credit history — older accounts help. This is why closing your first card is usually a mistake.
  4. New credit applications — each one is a hard inquiry. A burst of applications signals distress.
  5. Credit mix — a blend of revolving (cards) and installment (loans) history, all paid on time.

Notice what's missing: income, savings, job title. The score measures behaviour with borrowed money, nothing else.

Why is utilisation the fastest lever?

Illustrative example. You have one card with a ₹1,00,000 limit and a ₹80,000 balance — that's 80% utilisation, deep in the danger zone. You pay it down to ₹25,000: utilisation falls to 25%, under the 30% line. Of everything in this plan, crossing below 30% utilisation is the single fastest score lever most people have — and it requires no new credit, just a payment.

Two subtleties: utilisation is measured per-card and overall, so maxing one card hurts even if your total looks fine. And it has no memory — last month's 90% doesn't haunt you once this month reads 20%. That's what makes it fast.

What if my credit file is thin — or my score is very low?

If lenders won't give you a regular card, don't apply repeatedly — each rejection costs you a hard inquiry for nothing. Instead:

  • Take the secured-card path. A fixed-deposit-backed credit card reports to the bureaus exactly like a normal card. Use under 30%, autopay in full, and in 12–18 months you'll have a real file.
  • Become an add-on user the right way — only on a family member's old, clean, low-utilisation card. Their history helps; their mistakes would hurt.
  • Small consumer-durable EMIs paid flawlessly also build installment history — but only take one you actually need.

What should I never do during these 90 days?

  • Close your oldest card. It shortens your history and shrinks your total limit — utilisation jumps overnight.
  • Pay only minimums while spending more. Minimums keep the account "current" but the balance — and utilisation — keeps climbing.
  • Apply everywhere hoping something sticks. Five rejections in a month is worse than one careful application.
  • Pay a "score repair" agency. Accurate negatives can't be paid away; errors can be disputed by you, free, on the bureau's own site. The RBI licenses the bureaus — the dispute process is a right, not a service to buy.

Questions, answered

How fast can my CIBIL score improve?

First movement in 60–90 days of clean behaviour, especially from fixing utilisation. Big jumps — say 650 to 750+ — usually take 6–12 months. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

Does checking my own score hurt it?

No. Self-checks are soft inquiries and never affect the score. Only lender checks when you apply for credit count.

Do paid "score repair" agencies work?

No. Accurate negative information cannot be removed by paying anyone. Errors can be disputed by you directly with the bureau, free. That's the entire legitimate toolkit.

Should I close a credit card I don't use?

Usually not — especially your oldest. Closing shortens history length and cuts total available limit, pushing utilisation up. Keep it open with a small autopay charge instead.

What score do lenders want to see?

Scores run 300–900. Most lenders look for 750+ for smooth approvals and the best rates; the 700s often approve with tighter terms. Below 650, expect friction — which is what this plan fixes.

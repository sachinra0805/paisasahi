New vs old tax regime FY 2026-27: the one-table answer

Updated: October 2026 · Slabs verified October 2026 against Budget 2026 (no changes announced for FY 2026-27)

The short answer: for most salaried Indians in FY 2026-27, the new regime wins. Salaried income up to ₹12.75 lakh pays zero tax under it — ₹75,000 standard deduction plus a ₹60,000 rebate on taxable income up to ₹12 lakh. The old regime only wins if your total deductions (80C + HRA + 80D + home-loan interest combined) exceed roughly ₹5 lakh at mid incomes, ₹7–8 lakh at higher incomes. No home loan and modest HRA? Stop reading — it's the new regime.

What are the slabs for FY 2026-27?

FY 2026-27 is the year starting 1 April 2026 (assessed in AY 2027-28). Budget 2026 made no changes to slabs, rates, standard deduction, rebate, surcharge, or cess — the Budget 2025 structure continues. The new regime is the default; you opt into the old one.

Key differences beyond the slabs: standard deduction is ₹75,000 under the new regime vs ₹50,000 under the old (salaried). The new regime's 87A rebate (up to ₹60,000) applies to taxable income up to ₹12 lakh; the old regime's rebate (₹12,500) only covers income up to ₹5 lakh. Add 4% health & education cess on the tax in both. Full slab history and utilities are on incometax.gov.in.

How does the zero-tax limit actually work?

It's two parts stacking. First, the ₹75,000 standard deduction comes off your salary before slabs apply — so ₹12,75,000 gross becomes ₹12,00,000 taxable. Second, the rebate wipes out the entire slab tax (₹60,000 on ₹12 lakh) as long as taxable income stays within ₹12 lakh. Result: a salaried person earning up to ₹12.75 lakh in FY 2026-27 pays zero income tax under the new regime. Just above ₹12 lakh taxable, marginal relief keeps the extra tax from exceeding the extra income.

When does the old regime win?

The old regime's only weapon is deductions: 80C (₹1.5L), HRA exemption, 80D health insurance, home-loan interest up to ₹2L under 24(b), NPS, and more. We computed the breakeven — the deduction total at which the old regime starts beating the new — at several incomes:

The pattern: the breakeven rises with income. In practice, the old regime wins for people with a home loan (₹2L interest) plus large HRA plus full 80C — the classic metro homeowner profile. Everyone else: new regime.

Illustrative example — assumptions as above. ₹20 lakh salary, ₹4 lakh total deductions (80C ₹1.5L + HRA ₹2L + 80D ₹50k). New regime: taxable ₹19.25L → slab tax ₹1,85,000 → +4% cess = ₹1,92,400. Old regime: taxable ₹15.5L → slab tax ₹2,77,500 → +4% cess = ₹2,88,600. New regime wins by ₹96,200 — ₹4 lakh of deductions wasn't even close to the ~₹7.1 lakh breakeven at this income.

Questions, answered

Which regime is the default for FY 2026-27?

The new regime (Section 115BAC, now Section 202 of the Income-tax Act, 2025). If you make no choice in your return, the new regime applies — you opt into the old one explicitly.

I only claim 80C of ₹1.5 lakh. Which regime?

Almost certainly the new regime. ₹1.5 lakh is nowhere near the ~₹5 lakh-plus breakeven at mid incomes. Run the calculator to confirm, but don't be surprised.

Can I claim HRA under the new regime?

No. HRA exemption isn't available under the new regime — nor are 80C, 80D, or home-loan interest under 24(b). The new regime keeps the ₹75,000 standard deduction and employer NPS contributions, and that's nearly it.

Can I switch regimes every year?

Salaried individuals with no business income: yes, choose afresh each year when filing. With business income, opting out of the new regime restricts switching back — check the current ITR instructions or a CA.

Do senior citizens get different slabs?

Under the old regime, yes — the nil band rises to ₹3 lakh (60–79) and ₹5 lakh (80+). Under the new regime, slabs are identical at every age.
